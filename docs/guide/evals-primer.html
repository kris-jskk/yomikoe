Evals for YomiKoe: measuring how good, not only whether it works

YomiKoe turns a PDF into speech with word-by-word highlighting. Its tests say whether each part works. This page explains a proposed second layer, called evals, that says how well each part works and whether a change made it better or worse.

1

What is an eval?

"We already have tests. What is different about an eval?"

A test asks a yes-or-no question about one behaviour. Does the light switch turn the light on? It passes or it fails. Tests run on every change so nothing breaks by accident.

An eval (short for evaluation) asks a different question: how good is it, and compared with what? It runs the system over a fixed set of examples, gives each example a score, keeps every score, and summarises them. It saves the result next to the exact version of the code that produced it (a version is called a commit), so two runs can be compared later. An eval never fails on its own. It produces numbers and a difference between runs. A gate is an eval with a pass mark bolted on.

Analogy. A driving test is a test: you pass or you fail, and nobody records how close you came. A lap time is an eval: every lap gets a number, you keep them all, and you can see whether this week's car is faster than last week's.

Three ideas carry everything

2

Why YomiKoe needs them

"Everything passes. Why measure more?"

A review of the current code found six gaps. Each one means a change could make YomiKoe worse for a listener without any test noticing.

1. Scores exist but are thrown away

Reading order, sentence boundaries, an 11-check quality checklist, time-stretch accuracy, time to first audio, real-time factor and highlight drift are all computed today, then reduced to pass or fail. The last recorded reading-order mean was 0.9914, and nothing tracks it.

nobody can say whether a fix to the PDF reader (the parser) helped or hurt two-column papers, except by digging through old logs.
2. Silent slips inside the pass mark

Each PDF must score at least 0.95. A PDF can slide from 0.99 to 0.96 and every test still passes.

that slide is several sentences read in the wrong place in every document.
3. The speech layer has no quality figure

Turning numbers, units and abbreviations into words, deciding whether to spell out an acronym, and pronunciation are checked with a handful of hand-written yes/no examples. There is no accuracy figure over a larger collection.

"3 km" or "NASA" may be read wrongly, and no number would show it.
4. Audio is tested against a fake voice

The test voice (FakeBackend) produces silence with perfect word timings. It proves the plumbing, not the speech. Nothing compares the real voice model's (Kokoro's) word timings with its audio.

the highlighted word could drift away from the spoken word and only a person listening would catch it.
5. No yardstick for swapping the voice

YomiKoe is built so the voice model can be replaced. Without an eval there is no way to say a new one is better, faster or easier to understand than Kokoro on the same sentences.

a voice change would be chosen by impression, not by evidence.
6. Scanned pages are unmeasured

Scanned pages are read by optical character recognition (OCR). The 5 scanned sample PDFs are skipped on machines without the OCR program (tesseract). Even once it is installed, nothing will say how accurate it is.

a scanned book could be full of misread words with no warning.

4

The design

"So what would change?"

The core idea (proposed decision D31): an eval is a scored test with the pass mark removed and every per-example score kept. The scorers that already exist are reused unchanged. The eval layer adds only three things: a runner that calls them over the datasets and keeps every score, a store of saved run records, and a compare and report step. The existing gate tests keep their pass marks and still guard every change. Evals are the trend line above that floor.

Analogy. A spell-checker tells you a word is wrong. A reading-aloud exam scores how well a whole passage was read, and the teacher keeps last term's mark to compare. The gates stay the spell-checker; evals add the exam and the mark book.

Nine suites, grouped by what they cost to run

A suite is one dataset plus one scorer, answering one question. Each gets a new label, EVAL-1 to EVAL-9. The existing requirement labels stay untouched. The suites are sorted into three tiers by cost: how long they take and what they need.

Tier 0 · fastseconds · no voice model · every eval run
EVAL-1Order

Is the prose read in the right order?

20 sample PDFs with expected order · word-by-word comparison score · mean, worst, and per kind of document (academic, report, scanned, tables) scorer exists

EVAL-2Boundaries

Are sentences split where a reader would pause?

15 sentence files · precision, recall and F1 · mean F1, worst recall scorer exists

EVAL-3Clean text

How much junk (broken hyphens, stray dots, garbled ligatures) reaches the voice?

the same 15 sample files · the quality checklist counts, per 1 000 words · rate per check, total checks exist

EVAL-4Speech form

Does the text sent to the voice read aloud correctly?

a new list of ~300 pairs (written text → expected spoken words) covering numbers, dates, units, ordinals, abbreviations, acronyms, table cues and equations · exact match, with partial credit for near misses · accuracy overall and per category new

Tier 1 · with the voice modelminutes · real voice (Kokoro) on the processor · on demand or nightly
EVAL-5Intelligibility

Can the audio be understood?

50 sample sentences · round trip: the voice speaks, a speech recogniser (an MIT-licensed Whisper build) writes down what it hears, and word error rate compares the two · mean, worst sentence, per voice new; needs a licence check

EVAL-6Sync

Do the word timings match the audio?

the same 50 sentences · recogniser's word timings vs the voice's own timings: average gap in milliseconds and the share of words within 150 ms · per speed 0.5×, 1×, 2×, 3× new

EVAL-7Speed

How fast is speech produced on this machine?

20 benchmark sentences · time to first audio and real-time factor exists as a script

EVAL-8OCR

How accurate is the scanned-page path?

5 scanned sample PDFs · character error rate · mean, worst blocked until tesseract is installed

Tier 2 · a person listensa scheduled human session · saved in the same store
EVAL-9Listening

Does it sound good to a person?

10 sentences × 2 voices · a 1–5 score sheet for naturalness and pacing, plus a count of pronunciation errors · mean per voice, error count new

Where the files would live (proposed decision D32)

  • api/evals/the eval layer
  • runner.pyruns one suite or all of them, at a chosen tier, and writes a run record
  • suites/one file per suite, each saying what its dataset is, how to score one example, and how to summarise
  • data/the new speech-form pairs and the listening score sheet; the sample PDFs stay where they are
  • store.pysaves records named by date and short commit id, keeps a pointer to the latest, and compares two records
  • report.pyturns a comparison into a scorecard, as text and as a web page
  • evals/runs/the saved records, one small file per run

What one saved record looks like

Each run is saved as one small JSON file, a plain-text format for structured data that both people and programs can read.

{"run_id":"2026-10-17_7f2f015",
 "commit":"7f2f015",
 "date":"2026-10-17T14:02:00Z",
 "config":{"backend":"fake","speed":1.0,
           "tesseract":false},
 "suites":{"EVAL-1":{
   "examples":[{"id":"arxiv_2401_01234",
                "class":"academic",
                "score":0.993}],
   "aggregate":{"mean":0.991,"min":0.962,
     "by_class":{"academic":0.988}}}}}
  1. run_id names the run: its date plus the short code version.
  2. commit is the exact code version that was measured.
  3. date is when the run happened.
  4. config records the settings: which voice engine (here the fake one), the playback speed, and whether OCR was available.
  5. suites holds one entry per suite run. Under examples, each sample gets its own line with its id, its kind and its score.
  6. aggregate holds the summaries: the mean, the worst score, and a mean per kind of document.

The values in this record are the example shape from the design spec, not a measured run.

Compare and regressions (proposed decision D33)

Comparing two records lists, for each suite, how the summaries moved, and every example whose score fell by more than a set tolerance. The tolerance is 0.01 for scores between 0 and 1, 10% for timings, and 1 for error counts.

A regression is an example that got worse. Here it is a line in a report, not a failed test. The gate tests remain the only thing that can block a change from being merged. Later, once there is a baseline, a project rule could ask that every change explain any regression it causes.

When evals run, and what they will not do

A developer runs tier 0 before and after changing the parser or the speech wording, and tier 1 when touching the voice or swapping it. At the end of each milestone a full tier 0 and tier 1 run is saved and linked from the milestone guide. When a new voice engine is tried, EVAL-5, 6 and 7 run on the same sentences for both voices, and the scorecard becomes the decision document.

Two things are ruled out (proposed decision D34). No AI model grades the output in this first version; every scorer is a fixed rule or a person, so the same input always gets the same score. And no user data is used: the datasets are the committed sample files and sentence lists, never anyone's uploads.

Rollout

"In what order would this be built?"

In five phases, each with a clear check that it is done, following the project's habit of writing the acceptance check first.

  1. E0
    Runner and store
    The runner and the record store, with EVAL-1 and EVAL-2 wrapped.
    one run record is written, and comparing two identical runs shows zero change.
  2. E1
    Fast suites
    EVAL-3, and EVAL-4 with its ~300-pair dataset.
    all of tier 0 finishes in under 60 seconds, with accuracy printed per category.
  3. E2
    Model suites
    EVAL-5, 6 and 7 with the real voice; the speech recogniser passes the licence check.
    one tier 1 record on this machine, with word error rate and sync gap reported per speed.
  4. E3
    Reporting
    Text and web-page scorecards; a baseline rule added to the project instructions.
    a scorecard is linked from a milestone guide.
  5. E4
    Human and OCR
    The EVAL-9 listening sheet; EVAL-8 once tesseract is installed.
    one listening session is recorded.

Decisions taken

"What was decided before work started?" All three were answered yes on 2026-10-17: the EVAL labels and D31–D34 stand, Whisper is allowed as a development-only recogniser, and run records are committed to the repository.

  1. Accept the EVAL labels and decisions D31–D34 as proposed?Or fold evals into the existing requirement families instead of starting a new EVAL-n family.
  2. Allow a speech recogniser (Whisper, MIT licence) as a development-only tool?It would be used for EVAL-5 and EVAL-6 and never shipped to users. The alternative is to keep audio quality checks human-only for now.
  3. Commit saved run records to the repository?Keeping them in evals/runs/ makes history visible to everyone; the alternative is to keep them out of version control.

Glossary

Test
A check with a yes-or-no answer about one behaviour, run on every change.
Eval
A measurement that scores many examples, keeps every score, and compares runs over time.
Gate
An eval or test with a pass mark that must be met before work can be merged.
Dataset
The fixed set of example inputs an eval runs over, the same every time.
Ground truth
The known-correct answer for each example, prepared and checked by hand.
Fixture
A sample file kept in the project for testing, such as one of the 20 sample PDFs.
Scorer
The rule that compares an output with its ground truth and returns a number.
Baseline
An earlier saved run that a new run is compared against.
Regression
An example whose score got worse between the baseline and the new run.
Precision, recall, F1
Precision is the share of the sentence breaks found that are real, recall is the share of real breaks that were found, and F1 combines the two into one number.
Word error rate
The share of words a listener (here a speech recogniser) gets wrong compared with the intended text; lower is better.
Character error rate
The same idea counted in letters instead of words, used for OCR.
Real-time factor
Time spent producing audio divided by the length of that audio; below 1 means faster than listening speed.
Time to first audio
How long a listener waits between pressing play and hearing the first word.
